# Source Evaluation & Cross-Referencing Guide

## Source Types

| Type | Examples | Trust | Red Flags |
|------|----------|-------|-----------|
| **Primary** | Research papers, court filings, govt data, financial filings, legislation | Highest (check methodology) | Retracted, superseded, pre-print |
| **Secondary** | News articles, review papers, Wikipedia, analyst reports | Good for context; trace claims to primary | No citations, editorializing as fact |
| **Tertiary** | Blog summaries, social media, listicles, SEO content | Low — use as leads only | No author, no dates, no citations |
| **Opinion/Advocacy** | Op-eds, think tank pubs, industry white papers, campaign materials | Valuable for perspectives; don't treat as fact | Undisclosed funding, selective citation |

## CRAAP Test (Quick Version)

For each important source, assess:
- **Currency**: Published when? Still valid for this topic?
- **Relevance**: Actually addresses your specific question?
- **Authority**: Who wrote it? Credentialed? Writing in their expertise area?
- **Accuracy**: Claims supported by evidence? Verifiable? Methodology sound?
- **Purpose**: Inform, persuade, sell, or entertain? Conflicts of interest?

## Cross-Referencing Protocol

### 1. Independence Check
When multiple sources agree, verify they're actually independent:
- Do they cite the same original source? (Circular sourcing)
- Same organization or network?
- One clearly copied from the other?

**Circular sourcing**: Article A reports "X is 42%". Article B cites A. Article C cites B saying "multiple sources confirm." This is ONE source, not three.

### 2. Contradiction Analysis
When sources disagree, check:
- **Dates** — did something change?
- **Definitions** — measuring the same thing?
- **Methodology** — different methods yield different results
- **Scope** — same population/geography/timeframe?
- **Bias** — funding or affiliation explains the disagreement?

### 3. Source-on-Source
Does source A directly address source B's claims? Especially valuable for rebuttals, corrections, replication attempts, and expert reviews.

## Confidence Levels

| Level | Criteria |
|-------|----------|
| **High** | Multiple independent authoritative primary sources agree. Specific, verifiable claims. No credible contradictions. |
| **Medium** | Credible sources, limited corroboration. Minor explainable contradictions. Secondary sources citing primary data. |
| **Low** | Single source or limited evidence. Significant contradictions. Tertiary/opinion sources. Hard to verify. |
| **Flag** | Irreconcilable contradictions. Only advocacy sources. All sources trace to one unverified claim. |

## Common Traps

- **Consensus illusion**: Many articles ≠ independent confirmation. Check if they all trace to one press release.
- **Authority halo**: A Nobel laureate outside their field isn't an authority on that field. Evaluate the specific claim.
- **Recency bias**: Newer isn't always better. A well-designed 2019 meta-analysis > a 2026 preprint.
- **Specificity trap**: "47.3%" feels more credible than "about half," but specificity without methodology is meaningless.
- **Missing context**: A statistic can be true but misleading. Always ask: compared to what? Over what period? For which population?
