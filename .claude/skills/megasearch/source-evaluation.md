# Source Evaluation & Cross-Referencing Guide

## Source Classification

Every source you encounter falls into one of these categories. Know which you're looking at.

### Primary Sources
The original data, document, or firsthand account.
- **Examples**: Research papers, court filings, government statistics, official statements, raw datasets, patents, legislation text, financial filings (10-K, 10-Q)
- **Trust level**: Highest for factual claims. Still check methodology for research.
- **Red flags**: Retracted papers, superseded legislation, preliminary/pre-print data

### Secondary Sources
Analysis, reporting, or interpretation of primary sources.
- **Examples**: News articles, review papers, textbooks, analyst reports, Wikipedia
- **Trust level**: Good for context and interpretation. Always trace claims back to the primary source.
- **Red flags**: No citations to primary sources, editorializing presented as fact, cherry-picked data

### Tertiary Sources
Aggregations of secondary sources, or sources far removed from original data.
- **Examples**: Blog posts summarizing news articles, social media threads, listicles, SEO content farms
- **Trust level**: Low. Use only as leads to find better sources.
- **Red flags**: No author attribution, no dates, no citations, content feels generated/templated

### Opinion/Advocacy Sources
Sources with an explicit perspective or agenda.
- **Examples**: Op-eds, think tank publications, industry white papers, political campaign materials, advocacy organization reports
- **Trust level**: Valuable for understanding perspectives, but do NOT treat claims as factual without independent verification.
- **Red flags**: Undisclosed funding sources, selective citation, emotional language in place of evidence

## The CRAAP Test (Apply to Every Source)

### Currency
- When was this published or last updated?
- Is the information still valid for the topic?
- For fast-moving topics (tech, policy, markets): sources older than 1-2 years may be outdated
- For stable topics (history, mathematics, established science): older sources may be authoritative

### Relevance
- Does this source actually address your specific question?
- Is the information at the right level of depth?
- Would you be comfortable citing this source?

### Authority
- Who is the author? What are their credentials?
- What is the publishing body? Is it reputable?
- Is the author writing within their area of expertise?
- Can you verify the author exists and has the claimed credentials?

### Accuracy
- Are claims supported by evidence?
- Can you verify the factual claims independently?
- Is the methodology sound (for research)?
- Are there factual errors on things you CAN verify? (If yes, distrust everything else.)

### Purpose
- Why does this source exist? To inform? Persuade? Sell? Entertain?
- Is there a potential conflict of interest?
- Is the language objective or emotionally charged?

## Cross-Referencing Protocol

### Step 1: Identify Key Claims
For each source, extract the 2-3 most important factual claims. These are what you need to verify.

### Step 2: Independence Check
When multiple sources agree on a claim, check whether they're actually independent:
- Do they cite the same original source? (Circular sourcing)
- Are they from the same organization or network?
- Did one clearly copy from the other?

**Circular sourcing example:**
- Article A reports "Statistic X is 42%"
- Article B cites Article A: "According to reports, Statistic X is 42%"
- Article C cites Article B: "Multiple sources confirm Statistic X is 42%"
- This is ONE source, not three.

### Step 3: Contradiction Analysis
When sources disagree:

1. **Check dates** — Is one source more recent? Did something change?
2. **Check definitions** — Are they measuring/defining the same thing?
3. **Check methodology** — Different methods can yield different results legitimately
4. **Check scope** — Are they talking about the same population/geography/timeframe?
5. **Check funding/affiliation** — Could bias explain the disagreement?
6. **Check primary data** — Can you trace both claims back to raw data?

### Step 4: Source-on-Source Analysis
What does source A say about source B's claims? This is especially valuable when:
- A rebuttal directly addresses another source's methodology
- A correction or erratum is issued
- A later study explicitly tries to replicate or challenge an earlier one
- An expert review critiques another expert's work

## Confidence Scoring

After cross-referencing, assign each finding a confidence level:

### High Confidence
- Multiple independent, authoritative primary sources agree
- Claims are specific and verifiable
- No credible contradictory evidence
- Methodology is sound and transparent

### Medium Confidence
- Supported by credible sources but limited corroboration
- Some minor contradictions that can be explained
- Based on secondary sources that cite primary data
- Expert consensus with some dissent

### Low Confidence
- Single source or limited evidence
- Significant contradictions between sources
- Based on tertiary/opinion sources
- Claims are vague or hard to verify
- Topic is rapidly evolving

### Flag / Cannot Determine
- Sources contradict each other with no clear resolution
- Only advocacy/opinion sources available
- All sources trace back to the same unverified claim
- Evidence is insufficient to make any determination

## Common Traps

### The Consensus Illusion
Many articles about the same topic ≠ independent confirmation. Check if they all trace to the same press release, study, or wire service report.

### The Authority Halo
A Nobel laureate speaking outside their field is not an authority on that field. A prestigious institution can still publish flawed work. Evaluate the specific claim, not just the source's general reputation.

### The Recency Bias
Newer isn't always better. A well-designed 2019 meta-analysis may be more reliable than a 2026 preprint. Evaluate methodology, not just date.

### The Specificity Trap
A source that gives very specific numbers (e.g., "47.3% of users") feels more credible than one that says "about half." But specificity without methodology is meaningless. Always ask: how was this measured?

### The Missing Context
A statistic or claim can be technically true but deeply misleading without context. Always look for: compared to what? Over what time period? For which population?
